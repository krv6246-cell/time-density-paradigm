# How to use the concept responsibly

A useful concept should make observations clearer, not make claims bigger than the evidence. Time Density can be used as a small notebook for describing temporal situations.

## A four-step observation

Choose one ordinary situation: a meeting, a commute, a study session, or a software process. Write down:

1. **System:** What exactly are you observing?
2. **State:** What conditions, resources, and options are present?
3. **Transitions:** What changed, and how often?
4. **Temporal context:** What created pressure or made the sequence feel calm?

Do not try to produce a score at first. A precise description is more useful than a number whose meaning has not been defined.

## Compare, but do not overclaim

You can compare two sessions by recording the same observations each time. For example, note the number of interruptions, decisions, task switches, or unexpected events. These notes may reveal a pattern in that particular setting.

They do not automatically show a law of nature, a universal property of human perception, or a medical condition. A pattern needs a clear measurement plan and appropriate evidence before it becomes a research claim.

## Keep provenance visible

When adding an article, model, or number to this vault, record:

- where the idea came from;
- whether it is a definition, observation, hypothesis, or established result;
- which variables and assumptions it uses;
- what evidence supports it;
- where it may not apply.

This discipline protects the project from accidental certainty. It also makes review easier: another reader can see what to verify, revise, or remove.

## The practical goal

The immediate goal is modest: use Time Density to notice how changes, attention, and urgency are arranged in time. If the project later proposes a measurement, it should define the measure openly and test it against observations. Until then, the concept is most valuable as careful language.
